Research

I am a low-temperature and isotope geochemist in the Department of Geosciences at Princeton University. My work reconstructs the chemical composition of ancient seawater and uses it to test how ocean chemistry, climate, and the carbon cycle are coupled over geological time.

Most of what we know about the deep-time ocean comes from indirect proxies. I work instead with samples of the ocean itself: microscopic droplets of seawater trapped as fluid inclusions in marine halite, some of them more than 500 million years old. Reading them requires analytical methods that did not exist a decade ago, so a large part of my research is method development — combining laser-ablation ICP-MS with cryogenic sample handling to measure major, minor, and trace elements in a droplet a few tens of micrometres across.

Seawater chemistry from fluid inclusions in halite

I developed a technique combining LA-ICP-MS with cryo-SEM-EDS that makes quantitative major, minor, and trace element analysis of individual fluid inclusions routine. Applying it to more than 1,371 inclusions in over 131 halite samples from roughly 32 Phanerozoic and Neoproterozoic evaporite basins produced a direct record of how seawater calcium, sulfate, strontium, and lithium have varied, and showed that seafloor hydrothermal circulation — not continental weathering alone — sets the long-term budget of several of these elements.

Lithium isotopes and the long-term carbon cycle

Lithium isotopes track silicate weathering, the process that regulates atmospheric CO2 over millions of years. I have reconstructed the concentration and isotopic composition of lithium in seawater across the past 550 million years from fluid inclusions, and extended the record to roughly 3 billion years using marine dolostones. Mass-balance modelling of these records tests which mechanisms — weathering, hydrothermal exchange, or reverse weathering — actually drive the changes, and how they connect to Earth’s alternating greenhouse and icehouse states.

Chemical imaging and dating of old ice

As an NSF Office of Polar Programs Postdoctoral Fellow I am developing a chronometer based on the 87Sr/86Sr ratio of sea salts trapped in Antarctic blue ice, to date stratigraphically disturbed ice older than one million years. The same cryo-cell LA-ICP-MS approach produces high-resolution two-dimensional maps of impurities in folded ice, which reveal layering and stratigraphic orientation that visual inspection alone cannot resolve.

Brines, evaporites, and critical minerals

The processes that concentrate lithium, boron, and potassium in sedimentary basins are the same ones recorded in the evaporite archive. I study how these elements behave during extreme evaporation of seawater, why some basinal brines become lithium-rich, and what modern systems such as the Dead Sea and the Danakil Depression reveal about ancient hypersaline basins — work with direct bearing on where critical mineral resources form.

Methods and tools

LA-ICP-MS including cryo-cell configuration and LIBS, MC-ICP-MS, ICP-QQQ-MS, ICP-OES, cryo-SEM-EDS, electron microprobe, XRD, gravity-column chromatography, prepFAST, and fluid-inclusion heating–freezing stages. Modelling and analysis in MATLAB, SILLS, Iolite, EQL-EVP, PHREEQC, FREZCHEM, and Geochemist’s Workbench.
